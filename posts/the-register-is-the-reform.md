---
title: "The Register Is the Reform"
category: opinion
excerpt: "Enrolment campaigns and school-building budgets mean little if attendance is never counted. Nigeria's Universal Basic Education Act already requires daily attendance, but twenty-two years on, nobody enforces it."
---

Last Tuesday I went to the spare parts market in Kaduna. I had recently imported a 2016 Hyundai Sonata Sport, and it needed two things. The first was the plastic undertray that shields the engine bay from sand and road debris, which matters here more than it does in the US. The second was the emblem for the front bumper, which had gone missing somewhere between the port and my street.

The undertray is useful. The emblem is decoration. I spent most of that morning hunting for a small piece of chrome whose entire job is to make the car look finished.

I am telling you this because of what I saw while I was doing it.

It was Thursday morning, the second week of the school year. The market was full of boys. Not customers, and not apprentices learning a trade under a mechanic, though there were some of those too. I mean boys of nine, ten, twelve, hawking sachet water, carrying tyres, sitting on a bench with nothing to do at eleven in the morning on a school day. Later I drove to see family and passed more of the same on the street.

None of this was surprising. That is exactly what bothers me.

It has been our culture to have an explanation ready for it. The first weeks of term are always slow in this part of the country. The farm still needs hands in September. Parents wait to see whether the school is really open and really staffed before sending anyone. Uniforms, sandals and exercise books are bought across paydays that have not arrived. Everyone knows the pattern. Because everyone knows it, nobody calls it a failure. We plan the term around the assumption that attendance will thicken by October, more or less, and we move on.

I have come to think that assumption is the most expensive idea in northern education.

Look at what our governments are buying. Kaduna State says education takes a quarter of its 2026 budget, and points to school construction, classroom rehabilitation and teacher recruitment alongside free basic education and school feeding. The Reaching Out-of-School Children project, a four-year intervention valued at 62.8 million dollars and backed by the Islamic Development Bank, the Kuwait Fund, the Global Partnership for Education, Education Above All, Save the Children and UNICEF, is running across all 23 local government areas of the state. At the center, the education minister announced this month that more than a million out-of-school children have returned to classrooms over the past twenty months.

Those are serious commitments. Real money, real procurement, real political attention.

Now set them beside the one thing none of it survives without. A trained teacher standing in a furnished classroom in front of empty desks produces nothing. Not a weak result. Nothing. The textbook nobody opens is a cost with no return. The feeding programme that reaches a child on Monday and then not again until Thursday has fed a child, which is good, and has educated no one. Every input in an education budget is multiplied by attendance. When the multiplier falls, the entire investment falls with it.

This is why I want us to argue about attendance rather than enrolment.

Enrolment is a headcount taken on a day of the government's choosing. It is easy to report, easy to celebrate and easy to inflate. Attendance is measured every morning by a teacher who may or may not ever be checked. The gap between the two is where the failure hides.

Kaduna's household mapping exercise makes that gap unusually visible. The survey covered roughly 360,000 households and identified 187,719 out-of-school children. Of those, 60.2 percent had never enrolled. The remaining 39.8 percent had enrolled and then stopped. That second group is close to 75,000 children who had already sat in a classroom the state had already paid for, and who were then lost. Whatever went wrong for them went wrong after enrolment. No enrolment campaign was ever going to hold them.

The reasons are not mysterious, and they are not mainly about culture. More than nine in ten of the affected households pointed to poverty and household economic pressure. Boys make up about 57 percent of the children identified, many of them farming or hawking to keep a family going, which is precisely what I was looking at in the market while I shopped for an emblem. Nearly 65 percent are between six and eleven years old. These are small children doing adult arithmetic about whether their labour is worth more today than their schooling is worth in ten years.

So I am not arguing that we begin by prosecuting parents. I am arguing that we begin by counting properly and responding quickly, and leave sanction where it belongs, at the end of a chain that has already offered help.

Here is the part most people do not know. The law already says all of this. The Compulsory, Free Universal Basic Education Act of 2004 provides at section 2(2) that every parent shall ensure that his child attends and completes primary and junior secondary school. Then, in case that was insufficiently clear, section 4(1) provides that every parent shall ensure that his child receives full-time education by regular attendance at school. Regular attendance. Those words are in the statute and have been there for twenty-two years.

The penalties are there too. A first conviction earns a reprimand. A second carries a fine of two thousand naira or one month in prison. Subsequent convictions carry five thousand naira or two months. Those sums were modest in 2004, and inflation has since turned them into a joke. More to the point, enforcement of the Act on enrolment and retention has been effectively absent. A duty that nobody measures and nobody follows up is not a law. It is a sentence in a document.

It is worth adding that the same Act defines basic education to include almajiri and street children by name. They are not outside the scope of the law. They are inside it, and uncounted.

Taking section 4(1) seriously would look like three things. Daily registers that are actually collected, digitised and audited, rather than filled in retrospectively the week before an inspection. Attendance published by school, the way enrolment figures are published, so that a headteacher is visible on the one metric that constrains everything else. And a named person responsible for the child who misses a week, with the authority to find out why and a budget to remove the obstacle when the obstacle is money, distance, illness, or a father who needs an extra pair of hands until harvest.

That third element already exists here in prototype. The ROOSC project uses caseworkers to identify attendance problems and intervene when a child stops turning up, supported by community coalitions of traditional rulers, religious leaders, parents and school-based management committee members, with oversight running through the project unit, the state education board, the Ministry of Education and the Kaduna State Quality Assurance Authority. It runs an accelerated programme that compresses basic education into two and a half years for over-age learners aged eleven to seventeen. It integrates tsangaya centres into the system rather than fighting them. It has distributed menstrual health materials so that girls stop losing several days a month.

Project officials have cited a 2026 Save the Children learning report showing enrolment gains in participating schools. I have not been able to read that report, and until it is public, the honest position is that the attendance model is promising rather than proven. That gap is itself part of my argument. We are asked to accept education outcomes on the strength of briefings, which is exactly the habit that lets attendance go unmeasured in the first place. Publish the registers and the learning reports, and the argument settles itself.

What is already clear is that the mechanism exists, in this state, and that it is a donor-funded project in one state rather than the standing posture of every SUBEB in the North.

None of this settles the harder arguments about teacher quality, curriculum and what a child actually learns in the hours he is present. Those matter, and eventually they matter more. But they sit downstream. You cannot improve instruction for a child who is not there to receive it, and you cannot judge a school on learning outcomes when a third of its pupils are counted in September and seen again in November.

I got the emblem in the end. The car looks finished now, and the part that actually protects the engine is bolted on underneath where nobody sees it. We have built a great deal in this region that looks finished. The register is the part underneath. It is unglamorous, it photographs badly, no commissioner will ever cut a ribbon on one, and nothing else we spend money on works without it.

*Muhammad Nazif Abubakar<br>Kaduna, Nigeria*
